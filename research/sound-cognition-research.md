# Sound and cognition: what the evidence supports

*22 October 2026. The output of the research plan in `plan/research-plan-sound-cognition.md`.
Four clusters were each swept by a researcher, then attacked by a skeptic who reopened every
source. This document is the synthesis. It deliberately stops before working out what any of
this means for the engine: it states rules as far as the evidence carries them and names the
knob each rule touches, but proposes no changes. That step is for later, together.*

- **"Corrected" mostly means narrowed.** Skeptics removed numbers they could not reopen, fixed
  directions, and downgraded grades. One correction (C4-03) reversed a claim outright: the
  researcher's "music harms learning" was the opposite of what the source says. It is kept,
  with its direction rewritten, rather than rejected.
- **Several papers appear in more than one cluster.** Kämpfe 2011 (C1-27, C3-01, C4-02), Chmiel
  & Schubert 2017 (C1-21, C3-11, C4-21), Kiss & Linnell (C1-26, C3-09, C4-17), Nigg 2024
  (C3-22, C4-19), Woods 2024 (C3-24, C4-15), Mehta 2012 (C3-27, C4-11), the Mozart effect
  (C3-30, C4-14), Eno (C1-31, C4-22), Worrall (C1-32, C4-23), Schlittmeier 2012 (C1-02, C4-06),
  Hongisto 2017 (C2-21, C4-20), Thompson 2012 (C3-07, C4-08), Southwell 2017 (C1-15, C2-10),

- **The search budget ran out partway through every skeptic pass.** Later checks used direct

  graded strong on standing rather than on a source opened this session.

## The vocabulary this rests on

Four concepts from the literature carry most of the weight below.

- **Changing state.** A stream whose successive tokens differ spectrally (speech, a melody, a
  run of different hits) disrupts memory for order. A steady or repeated token does not. This
  is the *irrelevant-sound effect*.
- **Deviant.** An event that breaks what the listener's system predicted. It costs a moment of
  orienting away and back. What counts is the broken prediction, not rarity or novelty.
- **Salience.** How far an element sits from the statistics the scene has set up: its loudness
  step, brightness, roughness, pitch, location, timing.
- **Arousal.** The general activation lever. Tempo moves it most; mode and harmony move mood
  instead.

## 3. Conflicts

| Conflict | Findings | What would settle it |
|---|---|---|
| **Does music help or harm learning?** One meta-analysis finds a positive pooled effect (d = +0.31); the reading meta-analyses find small harms (g = −0.21; r = −.11). The positive one pools music played *before* the test with music *during* it. | C4-03 vs C4-01, C4-02, C3-01 | A reanalysis of de la Mora Velasco restricted to during-task music. |
| **Which way does the fluctuation-strength model misfit steady sounds?** C1-02's skeptic read "overestimated the (non-)disturbance" as *under*-predicting disruption; C4-06's skeptic read it as *over*-predicting. Ellermeier & Zimmer (C4-05) report a repeated syllable has no effect, which fits the second reading. | C1-02 vs C4-06 | Open the Schlittmeier full text (paywalled) and read the steady-state figure. |
| **Do people habituate?** Banbury & Berry 1997 found lab habituation with dishabituation after quiet; earlier Röer work found deviant habituation; Littlefair finds acoustic deviation habituates. Bell 2019 (N = 273, preregistered) found none for changing state or deviants; office workers report none. Possible reconciliation: acoustic deviants fade, changing state and categorical deviants do not. | C1-11, C1-04 vs C1-03, C4-05 | A preregistered study crossing acoustic vs categorical change with long continuous exposure. |
| **One mechanism or two?** The duplex account (changing state separate from attention capture) vs unitary, graded-attention accounts in which task difficulty also reduces speech disruption. | C1-01 | Not opened this session; the Buchner, Bell and Röer and Halin papers are the next read. |
| **Does preferred music help or hurt?** Liked music hurt serial recall more than disliked; preferred music raised task-focus on low-load vigilance. The tasks differ in load. | C4-07 vs C1-26, C4-17 | One design crossing preferred vs non-preferred music with high vs low load in the same people. |
| **Is the rising bias a hazard at low levels?** Rises are overestimated and act as warnings (Neuhoff, Bach), but at low intensities the bias reversed (Olsen & Stevens). | C1-09, C2-11 | Rising-vs-falling judgements and orienting measures at 40–65 dB with musical elements. |
| **Regularity: cheap or revealing?** Regular backgrounds distract less, but make newcomers easier to notice. Not a conflict in the data; a trade-off with no measured balance point. | C2-10, C1-15 | Entry detection vs task cost across levels of bed regularity. |
| **Same paper, different grades.** Thompson 2012 (fast and loud) is moderate in C3 (statistics read from the PDF) and weak in C4 (abstract only). C3's reading of the numbers stands; one piece and N = 25 limit it either way. | C3-07 vs C4-08 | An independent replication with more than one piece. |
| **Noise and creativity.** 70 dB helped adult creativity (Mehta); a child study near 64 dB found nothing; music with or without lyrics hurt remote associates; happy music helped divergent thinking. | C4-11, C3-27, C4-12, C4-13 | A preregistered replication of Mehta at headphone levels, separating divergent from convergent tasks. |
| **Do timbre deviations add?** Bouvier's Exp 5 results say additive; its own discussion says non-additive. | C2-04 | The paper's raw Exp 5 data. |
| **Introversion.** Cheah 2022 lists introverts as a moderator; Küssner (13 vs 21 results) and Avila (no interaction) say it is unreliable. | C3-02 vs C3-25, C4-18 | A preregistered, powered personality × sound study. |
| **Slow tempo.** 66 bpm slowed speeded responses (and raised verbal accuracy); slow music did not harm reading in Thompson. Different tasks and outcomes. | C3-08 vs C3-07 | A tempo sweep on one task measuring both speed and accuracy. |
| **Who benefits from 16 Hz AM?** The Woods paper's results text contradicts itself on which symptom group benefited. | C3-24, C4-15 | Independent replication; the authors' corrected analysis. |
| **Which incidental-repetition effect wins?** Incidental listening tolerates and even rewards repetition (liking rises), while exact recurring material is implicitly learned within minutes and kept for weeks. Neither study measures distraction or boredom over days. | C1-23, C4-21 vs C1-12, C1-16 | A multi-day study of fixed vs fresh material measuring boredom and task cost. |

## 4. False or unsupported popular claims

| Claim | Why it fails | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| "You get used to it." | No habituation of changing-state or deviant effects within or across sessions (N = 273); office workers report none. | C1-03, C4-05, C1-11 |
| "Turn it down and it stops distracting." | Disruption was the same at 60 and 75 dB(A); what matters is the changing sound's prominence over a steady floor. | C2-22, C4-05 |
| "Music you like helps you concentrate." | Liked music hurt serial recall more than disliked; lo-fi was rated helpful but had no credible effect. | C4-07, C4-04 |
| "Background music boosts performance" (or "always harms it"). | Overall effect is null; small harms for reading and memory; the one positive learning meta-analysis mixes pre-task music in. | C4-02, C4-01, C4-03 |
| "Attention lasts 10 minutes (or 8 seconds)." | Rests on repeated citation, not primary evidence. | C1-28 |
| "Work in 90-minute cycles (waking BRAC)." | The REM cycle is sleep-dependent; waking ultradian rhythms are weak and contested. | C1-29 |
| "Novelty or rarity is what grabs attention." | Capture comes from violated predictions. | C1-05, C1-06 |
| "Brief periodic breaks or cues reset vigilance." | No benefit in N = 498; Bayesian evidence for the null. | C1-25 |
| "Repeated exposure makes sounds more liked." | The exposure-liking curve appeared for visual, not auditory, stimuli. | C1-22 |
| "Predictable patterns grab attention." | Regularity does not capture; random distractors disrupt more. | C1-15, C2-10 |
| "Rising sounds always seem to change more (universal looming bias)." | The bias reversed at low intensities, and a loudness model explains it without a looming adaptation. | C2-11, C1-09 |
| "Nature sounds beat engineered noise." | No water sound beat pseudo-random noise in a 15-week office study; babbling water and birds were worse. Steadiness, not naturalness. | C2-21, C4-20 |
| "dB(A) measures comfort." | Unpleasantness tracks loudness, sharpness and roughness, not A-weighted level; A-weighting underweights low-frequency annoyance. | C2-16, C2-19 |
| "Low pure tones are the most annoying." | The tonal penalty was larger at 100–400 Hz than at 40–50 Hz. | C2-20 |
| "A fade-in of 200 ms or more prevents capture." | Rise-time numbers come from 105 dB startle and dolphins; nothing gives a rise time for low-level orienting. | C2-12 |
| "Crossfeed reduces headphone fatigue." | Practitioner assertion; no controlled study. | C2-26 |
| "Binaural beats entrain brainwaves: theta for calm, beta for focus." | No band-power, HR or skin-conductance change in a controlled study; 5 of 14 EEG studies consistent, 8 contradictory. | C3-21, C3-20 |
| "Mozart (or any composer) makes you smarter." | Small, bias-inflated effects explained by arousal and mood. | C3-30, C4-14, C3-03 |
| "60 bpm music slows your heart to match." | No study shows heart-rate entrainment to beats. | C3-06 |
| "Humanised, loose timing grooves more." | Quantised versions were rated as high or higher; residual microtiming had no effect. | C3-15, C3-14 |
| "A steady pulse sharpens attention (dynamic attending)." | Group-level replication failed in five experiments; N = 181 found no benefit. | C3-18, C3-17 |
| "The Yerkes-Dodson law: an arousal inverted U." | The 1908 study was about shock strength and habit formation; the arousal version is a later recasting. | C3-10 |
| "White, pink or brown noise helps everyone focus." | Small benefit only for ADHD or high-symptom youth; small cost for others; no brown-noise studies exist. | C3-22, C4-19 |
| "Noise effects follow a neat inverted U." | Helps 2014 failed to find one in any group. | C3-23 |
| "Introverts need silence, extraverts busy music." | As much evidence against as for; a direct test found no interaction. | C3-25, C4-18 |
| "Moderate noise (70 dB) boosts creativity: the coffee-shop effect." | One adult study; a child study near 64 dB found nothing. | C3-27, C4-11 |
| "Faster music speeds you up." | 138 bpm gave no gain over silence. | C3-08 |
| "Endel (generative) beats playlists." | In the under-36 subgroup, Spotify and Apple playlists also beat silence; industry-funded; EEG-decoded outcome. | C4-16 |
| "Brainwave phase-locking means better work." | Neural coupling shown; the behavioural benefit is a marginal, conflicted subgroup effect. | C4-25, C4-15 |

## 5. Gaps the literature does not answer

**Rates and durations.**
- No safe rate of gradual change (dB, semitones or density per minute) below which drift in a background goes unnoticed. Slow-change deafness gives one size, with no ramp rate.
- No fade-in or rise time measured against capture at background levels (about 40–65 dB), and no level-rise rate below which a rise stops acting as a warning.
- No evidence on the best tide period, or periodic vs 1/f slow modulation, over hours.
- No time course for how fast tempo, density or brightness can move in a state change without an orienting response.

**Session length.**
- Almost every study runs 4–30 minutes. None tests performance, fatigue, boredom or vigilance over 2–8 hour sessions, or whether music changes the time-on-task decline (a music × time interaction).
- Whether arousal effects of tempo or density persist or fade over hours is unknown.
- No controlled study of listening fatigue from hours of low-level headphone listening, stereo width or in-head localisation.

**Repetition and vocabulary.**
- No optimal pattern vocabulary size; the token-set-size work could not be read beyond its title.
- Whether a new seed each day helps or hurts is untested, though implicit pattern memory lasts 7 weeks.
- Repetition fatigue in background (not focal) listening is not quantified; game-audio sources are qualitative.
- No IC or entropy target for "unnoticed but not boring": IC and entropy are tied to attentive liking only.

**Comfort.**
- No validated sharpness or roughness ceiling for hours of music; the 1.75 acum figure is a convention.
- Low-frequency annoyance and tonal-penalty data come from ventilation and environmental noise, not bass-led music beds or deliberate drones on headphones.
- No validated salience model for music-like beds (best natural-scene AUC about 0.74).
- Grouping cues are measured with brief lab tones; their transfer to multi-minute beds, and how they combine, is unstudied.

**Tasks.**
- No controlled study of sound during programming, debugging or code reading.
- No study of sound during conceptual combination or analogy (the Fuse work); ideation evidence is conflicting single studies.
- Maths evidence is thin and mostly null.
- No study varies generative parameters (event rate, loop period, change rate, harmonic density) against task performance; Thompson 2012 is the nearest, with one piece.
- No optimal tempo or event rate for sustained cognitive work, and no evidence that moderate groove helps or hurts focus.
- No study compares a generative focus engine with a well-chosen instrumental playlist on objective output.

**People.**
- No validated way to place a listener on the arousal curve; introversion and ADHD are weak or contested proxies, and physiological targeting is untested.
- The noise benefit for ADHD rests on brief child lab tasks with white noise; adults, hours, brown noise, or noise under music are unknown.
- No tolerable thresholds (level, onset slope, transient rate) for autistic listeners; adult data are sparse.
- No dose-response for bed level in typical adults below lab levels.
- Whether choice and control offset the cost of background sound is unresolved.

## 6. Open questions worth a personal test

Each is a Lab trial against Deep Work: A vs B, one thing changed. Two notes apply to all of
them. First, self-report is weak evidence here (C4-04): pair "how it felt" with something
counted, such as recall after reading, words written, or task-focus probes. Second, rule 1
says novelty does not wear off, so a single session per arm is enough to see a cost but not
to rule one out.

| # | Contrast (A vs B) | Held constant | What you'd notice | Rests on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Deep Work vs silence, on a Read session | Passage difficulty, time of day, duration | Whether recall of what you read differs. The evidence predicts roughly no difference for an instrumental bed; a clear cost would mean the bed carries speech-like change. | C4-01, C4-04 |
| 2 | Deep Work at its usual level vs the same at −6 dB, on a memory-heavy task | Everything but level | The literature predicts no change in disruption. If quieter feels clearly better, comfort (not disruption) is what level is buying. | C2-22, C4-05 |
| 3 | Deep Work vs Deep Work with the beat dropped out (bed alone) for a stretch | Bed, level, session length | Whether the drop-out settles you, as full silence did in Bernardi, or registers as an event. | C3-05, C1-06 |
| 4 | Deep Work with the same seed every day for a week vs a new seed each day | Variation, level, job | Whether familiarity turns into boredom, comfort or nothing over days. | C1-16, C1-12, C1-23 |
| 5 | Deep Work on a 2–3 hour Hours session vs silence | Task type, length | Where in the session the difference shows (early, late, never). No study has run this length. | C1-24, C1-26, gaps |
| 6 | Deep Work vs Deep Work with its noise floor removed (or added) | Everything else | Whether a noise floor helps or costs you specifically; the population split is ADHD vs typical. | C3-22, C4-19 |
| 7 | Deep Work quantised vs with 1/f-humanised timing | Pattern, tempo, level | Whether you can tell, and whether either feels more "present". Evidence favours quantised. | C3-15, C3-16 |
| 8 | Deep Work vs Deep Work at a tempo nearer the groove band (about 110–120 bpm) | Pattern, level, bed | Whether motor pull rises and focus falls; groove's effect on work is untested. | C3-12, C3-13 |
| 9 | Deep Work vs a well-chosen instrumental playlist you like | Level, task, duration | Whether generative beats a playlist on counted output, the comparison nobody has run. | C4-16, gaps |
| 10 | Deep Work on a convergent task (a puzzle or a hard bug) vs silence | Task, time of day | Whether the remote-associates cost of music appears for you. | C4-12 |
| 11 | Deep Work told "this evolves slowly" vs not told | The sound itself | Whether being told makes you notice the drift more, as alerting did for slow change deafness. | C1-08 |
